The STAR method is usually taught as interview prep. It's just as useful in a performance review, where the problem is the same: you need to describe one piece of work so that someone who wasn't there understands what happened and why it mattered.

This guide shows how STAR fits a self-review, with six examples from different roles you can adapt.

## What STAR means

- **Situation**: the context. What was going on?
- **Task**: what needed to happen, and what part was yours?
- **Action**: what you did. This is the heart of it.
- **Result**: what changed because of it.

In interviews, people often spend most of their time on the situation. In a self-review your manager already knows the context, so keep Situation and Task short and spend your words on Action and Result.

## How STAR in a review differs from STAR in an interview

| | Interview | Performance review |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Reader | A stranger | Your manager, who knows the context |
| Situation | Needs setup | One sentence is enough |
| Proof | Your word | Can be checked, so it must be accurate |
| Length | Spoken, 1-2 minutes | Written, 3-5 sentences |

Because your manager can check the details, accuracy matters more than drama. A modest true result beats an impressive invented one.

## Six STAR examples for performance reviews

These are illustrative examples. Replace the details with your own and keep only numbers you can show.

### 1. Operations: fixing a slow approval process

> **Situation:** Vendor invoices needed sign-off from three teams and approvals often stalled in email.
> **Task:** I was asked to make approvals predictable before month-end close.
> **Action:** I mapped where invoices got stuck, built a single approval sheet with owners and due dates, and walked each approver through it.
> **Result:** Approvals moved to one place and finance stopped chasing sign-offs by email; average approval time went from about [X] days to about [Y].

### 2. Product: shipping under a late change

> **Situation:** Two weeks before the pricing page relaunch, legal asked for copy changes.
> **Task:** Keep the launch date without skipping the legal review.
> **Action:** I re-sequenced the design work, set a daily 15-minute check-in for the final two weeks, and got legal's sign-off on each section as it was ready instead of at the end.
> **Result:** The page launched on the planned date with legal approval.

### 3. Engineering: a recurring failure

> **Situation:** The nightly export job failed a few times a week and someone had to rerun it by hand.
> **Task:** Find the cause and stop the manual reruns.
> **Action:** I traced the failures to a timezone bug in the date filter, fixed it and added a regression test.
> **Result:** The job has run without manual reruns since the fix.

### 4. Customer success: saving an at-risk account

> **Situation:** A long-time customer's usage dropped and their renewal was coming up.
> **Task:** Understand why and make the renewal decision easier for them.
> **Action:** I set up a call with their new admin, found they'd never been trained on the reporting feature, ran two short training sessions and shared a one-page guide.
> **Result:** Their team started using reports again and they renewed.

### 5. Marketing: fixing a broken handoff

> **Situation:** Leads from webinars sat for days before sales saw them.
> **Task:** Get webinar leads to sales faster.
> **Action:** I worked with sales ops to route webinar sign-ups straight into the CRM with an owner assigned, and set up a weekly check of unassigned leads.
> **Result:** Webinar leads now reach a sales rep the same day.

### 6. Any role: onboarding a teammate

> **Situation:** Our team hired two people in the same month and had no written onboarding.
> **Task:** I volunteered to get them productive without pulling the whole team into training.
> **Action:** I wrote a first-week checklist and context doc, paired with each of them for an hour a day in week one, and collected their questions into an FAQ.
> **Result:** Both took on their own work in their second week, and the checklist is now used for every new hire.

## Fill-in STAR template for your self-review

```
[Short title of the work]

Situation: [One sentence of context]
Task: [What needed to happen and what part was yours]
Action: [What you did, in order. Use "I".]
Result: [What changed. Real number if you have one, plain description if not.]
```

## Three common mistakes

1. **"We" everywhere.** Your manager wants to know your part. Say "we" for the team effort and "I" for what you did.
2. **A result that is really an action.** "Launched the new flow" is an action. The result is what happened after: fewer tickets, faster setup, a renewal.
3. **Made-up metrics.** If you write "improved efficiency by 40%", be ready to show where 40% came from. If you can't, describe the change instead.

The best STAR stories come from notes written the week it happened, when you still remember the details. A sentence or two each Friday is enough raw material.
