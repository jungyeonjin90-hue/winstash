A weekly status update is a short note to your manager that says what you finished, what's next, and where you're stuck. Done well, it takes five minutes to write, keeps your 1:1 for real conversation, and quietly builds the record you'll need when your self-review is due.

Below is a template you can copy, examples for three kinds of roles, and the mistakes that make managers skim past updates.

## The weekly status update template

Copy this into an email, a chat message or the top of a running doc:

```
Weekly update: [week of Month Day]

Done
- [Outcome] by [what you did]. ([link or number if you have one])
- [Outcome] by [what you did].

Next week
- [Priority 1]
- [Priority 2]

Blockers / risks
- [What's stuck], waiting on [who or what]. Need by [date].

Need from you
- [One decision, intro or review you need from your manager]
```

Four sections are enough. If a week has no blockers, delete the section instead of writing "none".

## What to put in each section

### Done

This is the section your manager will reuse, so write it in a way they could forward without editing. Start with the result, then how you got there.

| Instead of | Write |
| --- | --- |
| Worked on the onboarding doc | New support hires now have one onboarding checklist; ran the first session with it |
| Meetings with legal | Got legal sign-off on the new pricing page copy |
| Fixed bugs | Fixed the export job that failed overnight; added a test so it doesn't come back |

If you have a number, include it. If you don't, describe what changed in plain words. Don't guess a number to make a line look stronger. A made-up figure is the one thing you'll have to defend later.

### Next week

Two or three priorities, in order. This is where your manager can redirect you before you spend a week on the wrong thing, which is the main reason to write it.

### Blockers and risks

Name what is stuck, who or what you're waiting on, and when it starts to hurt. "Waiting on finance for the Q4 budget numbers; the forecast slips if they don't arrive by Thursday" gives your manager something to act on. "Some delays" doesn't.

### Need from you

One specific ask: a decision, an introduction, a review, air cover. Managers like updates that make it obvious how they can help.

## Weekly status update examples

These are illustrative examples. Swap in your own work and only use numbers you can back up.

### Operations / BizOps

```
Weekly update: week of Oct 13

Done
- Vendor invoices now go through one approval sheet, so finance no longer chases sign-offs by email.
- Closed out the September headcount report and shared it with the finance lead.

Next week
- Draft the Q4 vendor renewal list
- Start the travel policy FAQ

Blockers / risks
- Waiting on IT to add two approvers to the sheet. Needed before month-end close.

Need from you
- 15 minutes to review the renewal list before it goes to finance.
```

### Product manager

```
Weekly update: week of Oct 13

Done
- Pricing page relaunch shipped on the planned date after legal's copy changes.
- Ran three customer calls on the reporting feature; notes and themes in the research doc.

Next week
- Write the one-pager for reporting v2
- Align with design on the empty-state flow

Blockers / risks
- Analytics event for plan upgrades is missing, so we can't measure the relaunch yet. Eng ticket filed.

Need from you
- A decision on whether reporting v2 or the onboarding fix goes first next quarter.
```

### Software engineer

```
Weekly update: week of Oct 13

Done
- Found and fixed the timezone bug behind the nightly export failures; added a regression test.
- Reviewed and merged the new auth middleware with the platform team.

Next week
- Start the search indexing migration
- Pair with the new hire on the billing service

Blockers / risks
- Staging database is out of disk; migration testing is paused until infra resizes it.

Need from you
- Help getting infra to prioritize the staging resize.
```

## Five mistakes that make managers skim

1. **Listing activity instead of outcomes.** "Had meetings about X" tells your manager how you spent time, not what changed.
2. **Burying the blocker.** Put the thing that needs attention where it can't be missed, not in the fourth paragraph.
3. **Writing for yourself.** Skip internal shorthand and ticket numbers without context. Your update may be forwarded.
4. **Sending it irregularly.** An update that arrives on Friday afternoon every week becomes part of your manager's routine. A random one gets lost.
5. **Inflating it.** "Significantly improved" and invented percentages make every other line less believable.

## Your weekly updates are your review prep

The hardest part of a self-review is remembering what you did in February. If you've sent a weekly update every Friday, that problem is mostly solved: your year is already written down, in your own words, with dates.

A few habits make that record more useful later:

- Keep all updates in one place (a running doc or one email thread) instead of scattered chat messages.
- Write the result while you still remember it. The numbers and names are gone a month later.
- Mark the lines you'd want in your review with a star as you go.

When review season comes, read back through your updates and pull the starred lines into a brag sheet.
