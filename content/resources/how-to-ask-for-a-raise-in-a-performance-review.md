Waiting until your performance review meeting to ask for a raise is one of the most common timing mistakes professionals make.

At many companies, merit budgets, bonus pools, and band adjustments are locked weeks **before** managers sit down to deliver formal review ratings. If you bring up pay for the first time after seeing your score, your manager often has zero budget left to move—even if they agree you deserve more.

The highest-success approach starts **before calibration closes**: prepare a concise one-page record of your expanded scope, anchor it with objective market context, and have a collaborative conversation with your manager early.

## Before and after: framing a raise request around value

These are illustrative examples. Replace the details with your own work and only use numbers you can back up.

| Weak (needs-based or vague) | Strong (evidence-based and commercial) |
| --- | --- |
| I've been here two years and living costs went up, so I'm hoping for a raise this review cycle. | Over the past year my scope expanded to owning enterprise customer onboarding and training two new reps, while market ranges for this expanded scope sit higher than my current pay. |
| I worked really hard and hit my targets, so I think I deserve more money. | Across H1–H2, I delivered all three priority launches on date and built the intake process that cleared a multi-team bottleneck. Based on that trajectory, I'd like to discuss moving my base salary toward [Target]. |
| Can you give me whatever bump is standard this year? | Ahead of merit planning, I'd love to calibrate on what target range would align my compensation with the lead-level scope I've been carrying since Q2. |
| Other companies are paying people with my skills much more right now. | Looking at recent compensation data for [Role] in [Location/Market] alongside my ownership of [Project A and Project B], I want to align on a path toward [Target Figure / Range]. |

## The 4-step raise preparation playbook

### 1. Start the conversation early (4–6 weeks before ratings finalize)
Check your company's review calendar. Ask for a dedicated 20-minute agenda item in a regular 1:1 while managers are still building review packets and compensation recommendations.

### 2. Build a one-page "Scope & Proof" summary
Managers need ammunition they can forward upward to their director and HR Compensation partner. Include:
- **Role expansion since your last pay change**: new responsibilities, cross-team initiatives, or direct mentoring you took on that were not in your original job description.
- **3–5 concrete outcomes**: verified projects shipped, workflows improved, or recurring risks eliminated. (Keep only metrics you can point to in company docs.)
- **Market anchor**: two or three credible compensation benchmarks for your role, level, and geography.

### 3. State a clear number or tight range calmly
Speak neutrally and constructively—treat it as a joint planning problem, not an ultimatum. Aim slightly above your minimum acceptable number, leaving room for discussion while staying clearly within market reality.

### 4. Agree on follow-up milestones in writing
Whether your manager says "yes, I'll advocate for that," "let me check with HR," or "budgets are tight right now," summarize the discussion in a brief email afterward so expectations and review dates don't drift.

## Exact scripts and templates you can adapt

### Script 1: Pre-review 1:1 opener (before budgets lock)

```
"Hi [Manager Name], since annual review prep is starting in a few weeks, I wanted to reserve 15 minutes today to talk about my growth and compensation over the past year.

Since my last adjustment, my role has grown from [original baseline] to owning [major project / cross-team function / mentoring]. I put together a short one-pager of those outcomes alongside market benchmarks for similar scope in [city/remote band], and I'm aiming for a base around [$X / +X%] in this upcoming cycle.

How does that align with how you're viewing my performance, and what else would make the case strongest for calibration?"
```

### Script 2: One-page compensation follow-up email

```
Subject: Summary of 1:1 — Scope & compensation alignment ([Your Name])

Hi [Manager Name],

Thanks for taking the time to discuss my performance and compensation path today. Here is the quick recap of what we reviewed for reference ahead of calibration:

Key Scope Growth & Outcomes This Year:
- [Project/Area 1]: Owned [end-to-end deliverable], resulting in [verifiable team/business impact].
- [Project/Area 2]: Resolved [recurring bottleneck or customer issue], saving [team/stakeholder impact].
- [Multiplier]: Mentored [teammates] / created [process doc] now used across [team].

Target Alignment:
- Moving base compensation toward [$Target] (aligned with [Role/Level] benchmarks and expanded responsibilities).

Please let me know if any extra detail from my projects would help you during calibration. Looking forward to checking back in on [agreed date].

Best,
[Your Name]
```

### Script 3: If your manager says "Budgets are frozen right now"

```
"I appreciate you being upfront about the budget freeze. I really enjoy working with this team and want to stay on a strong growth path here. Could we outline the two or three specific scope milestones that would support a mid-year adjustment—or a move to [Next Level]—and put a check-in on the calendar for [Month]?"
```

## Common mistakes that sink raise requests

- **Leading with personal bills**: Mortgage, rent, or inflation explain why you want more money, not why the company should pay more for the role. Keep your rationale anchored in scope and market value.
- **Using another offer as a bluff**: Never threaten to leave unless you already hold an offer you are genuinely ready to accept.
- **Vague bragging without receipts**: Adjectives like *"worked tirelessly"* don't persuade finance teams; documented deliverables in your [running brag sheet](/resources/brag-sheet-template-for-work) do.
